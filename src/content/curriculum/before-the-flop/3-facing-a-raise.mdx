The player under the gun raises. You're on the button with ace-jack offsuit. An ace and a picture card — surely you're not folding?

Watch what you're actually up against. An under-the-gun raise means the tightest range in the game: big pairs, ace-king, ace-queen, the best suited hands. Now play ace-jack against that lineup. Every ace that calls you down has you beat. When you make a pair of jacks, ace-jack is fine — but when the ace comes and they bet three times, you're the one paying them off.

That situation has a name: domination. Your hand shares its best card with their hands, but loses the tie. Dominated hands don't lose small — they make second-best pairs that feel too good to fold.

## Your three options, honestly ranked

When someone has raised in front of you, calling is not the default. Here's the actual pecking order.

Fold is the quiet winner. Most hands that look tempting against a raise — ace-ten, king-jack, queen-ten — are dominated by the raiser's range and should go in the muck. Folding costs you nothing. It's the one move at the table with no downside.

The 3-bet — re-raising the raiser — is your aggressive option. You 3-bet your monsters: queens, kings, aces, ace-king. And you mix in some hands that play well when called but aren't strong enough to just call — suited aces like ace-five suited are the classic. Now you can win two ways: they fold, or you flop something worth stacking off with.

Calling with position is often the largest continuing band on the chart — not a timid leftover. You call with hands that are clearly profitable but don't want to bloat the pot: middle pairs hoping to flop a set — three of a kind using both your cards — and the best suited, connected hands. The 3-bet stays reserved for monsters and a few suited-ace bluffs.

## The question that does the work

Before you ever call a raise, ask: who raised, and from where? A raise from under the gun is a tight, scary range. The same raise from the button covers nearly half the deck. Ace-jack offsuit is a fold against the first and a call against the second — not a comfortable 3-bet. The raise size is the same. The seat behind it changes everything.

## Why the 3-bet slice is thin

New players get the middle backwards. They see a pretty hand and want to "put the pressure on" — but on this chart, calling is the larger continuing band and the 3-bet is the small premium-and-bluff mix. When you call a raise with position, you keep the pot manageable and play with the best seat at the table. When you 3-bet, you take the hand back: you set the price, you represent the strongest range, and you can win without seeing a card — so you save that for hands that actually want that job.

Fold the hands that can't fight. Call the hands that want a flop with position. 3-bet the monsters plus a few suited aces that play well when called. The middle that gets punished is inventing a 3-bet with a dominated offsuit broadway.

<KeyIdea>
  Against a raise, fold the pretty-but-dominated hands, 3-bet your strongest ones plus a few suited
  aces, and flat call the hands that truly want a flop — especially with position.
</KeyIdea>
