You're in the big blind with seven-six suited. The button raises to two and a half blinds. Last lesson said to be picky about calling raises. So this is a fold... right?

This time it's a call — and understanding why will change how you see every big blind you ever play.

## You're shopping at a discount

Here's the thing the big blind changes: your blind is already in the pot. It's not yours anymore — but it means you get a discount on the call.

Run the numbers. The button makes it 2.5 blinds. You already posted one, so the call costs you 1.5 more. The pot holds their 2.5, your 1, and the small blind's half: four blinds. You're paying 1.5 to win a four-blind pot — about 27 cents on the dollar. That price is called pot odds: what a call costs against what the pot pays.

At that price, your hand only needs to win about a quarter of the time to break even. Seven-six suited does better than that against a button's wide range. So the call makes money, even though the hand looks small.

One more edge: you close the action. Nobody can re-raise behind you and blow you off the discount. The small blind never gets that guarantee — which is one reason it defends far tighter than you do.

## How wide is wide?

Wider than feels reasonable, the first time you see it:

<RangeGridEmbed
  node="BB:vs_rfi_BTN"
  caption="Defending the big blind against a button raise. About four in ten hands continue."
/>

Against a button open, about four in ten hands defend — wide by seat, not "most of the deck." Suited anything-reasonable, connected cards, every pair, most aces. Against an under-the-gun raise the discount is the same but the opponent's range is far stronger, so the defense tightens sharply. The price stays fixed; the value of your hand against their range is what moves.

## The two mistakes to avoid

Beginners get the big blind wrong in both directions. Some fold everything but premiums, donating their discount hand after hand — the single most common leak in small-stakes poker. Others hear "defend wide" and call with junk like nine-three offsuit, then bleed away chips on flops they never hit. The discount is generous. It is not infinite.

And remember the discount ends preflop. Defending wide is right; falling in love with a weak pair after the flop is a different, expensive mistake. You bought a cheap flop — not a ticket to the river.

<KeyIdea>
  Your big blind is a discount coupon: defend far wider than you'd call anywhere else — then let the
  hand go when the flop misses you.
</KeyIdea>
