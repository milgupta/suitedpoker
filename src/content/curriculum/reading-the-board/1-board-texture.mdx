On the first board, your pair of kings is a fortress. On the second, it's a hand under siege. Learning to see that difference at a glance is called reading board texture — and it's the skill that makes every postflop decision easier.

## Dry boards: nothing is coming

King-seven-two with three different suits — called rainbow — is a dry board. Dry means disconnected: no two cards close in rank, no two of a suit. Nobody has a straight. Nobody has a flush. Nobody is even drawing to one yet.

On a board like this, the hand that's best now will usually still be best at the river. There are almost no draws — hands that need another card to become strong — so the next card rarely changes who's winning. Your top pair can bet small, get called by worse, and rarely fears the turn.

## Wet boards: everything is coming

King-queen-jack with two hearts is a wet board — coordinated cards, close in rank, sharing suits. Count the danger: any ten or ace threatens a straight, any heart threatens a flush, and hands like ace-ten or queen-jack have already connected hard. The strength of every hand is unstable, one card from changing.

On wet boards, the best hand right now is fragile. That flips your job: strong hands bet bigger — to charge the draws and protect what they have — and medium hands tread carefully, because by the river a lot of them stop being good.

## The in-between boards

Most flops aren't at either extreme, and that's fine — texture is a dial, not a switch. Ace-nine-four with two hearts is dry in rank but wet in suit: nobody has a straight, but the flush draw is live and the turn matters. Jack-ten-four rainbow is the reverse: connected enough that straight draws exist, with no flush to worry about.

Read the two dimensions separately and the middle cases stop being confusing. Ask about rank connection, then about suits, and let each answer push your sizing up or down on its own.

## The glance that becomes a habit

When a flop lands, ask two questions before anything else. Are the cards close together in rank? Do any share a suit? Two nos: dry board — made hands rule, small bets do the work, and the turn card rarely scares you. One or two yeses: wet board — draws are everywhere, strong hands should bet big now, and marginal hands should stay humble.

Do this on every flop you ever see, including the hands you've folded. It takes two seconds, and within a week it stops being a checklist and becomes just... seeing.
