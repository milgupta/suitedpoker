"I put him on ace-king."

You'll hear this at every table. A player squints, decides their opponent holds exactly ace-king, and plays the rest of the hand against that one guess. When the guess is wrong — which is most of the time — every decision built on it is wrong too.

Strong players never guess one hand. They track all of them at once. This is the single biggest mental shift in poker, and once it happens you can't go back.

## Every player is a chart, not a hand

You've already been using ranges for two modules — every preflop chart you've studied is one. The shift is realizing your opponent is a chart too. When the button raises, they aren't "ace-king or bluffing." They're this:

Every hand in that grid raised the same way. Until they act again, your opponent is all of those hands simultaneously — a few big pairs, a lot of medium stuff, plenty of suited junk. You're not playing against two hidden cards. You're playing against a weighted crowd.

## Every action shrinks the crowd

Here's what makes range thinking practical: each street, their actions erase parts of the grid.

They raised the button: the grid above. They bet the king-high flop: erase nothing yet — they'd bet most hands here. They bet big again on a blank turn: now erase the junk; the crowd shrinks to kings, better pairs, and a modest set of draws. They shove the river when the flush comes in: the crowd is now flushes, sets... and the rare bluff.

Notice what you never did: guess. You just kept subtracting. By the river the question isn't "do they have it?" — it's "how much of what's left beats me?" That's a counting problem, not a mind-reading problem.

## How to practice this without a headache

Nobody tracks all one-hundred-plus hand types in real time. You track buckets. After each action, sort their range into three: hands that beat you, hands you beat that keep paying, and draws. Rough counts are fine — "mostly worse pairs, a few monsters" is a range read, and it's ninety percent of the value.

Do it out loud when you review your sessions. "What raises there? What calls the flop? What shoves?" The day you catch yourself asking those questions mid-hand instead of "what do they have?" — that's the day this lesson finished installing.
