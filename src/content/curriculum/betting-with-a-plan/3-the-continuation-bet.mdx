You raise before the flop with ace-queen. The big blind calls. The flop comes king-eight-three, missing you completely — and your opponent checks.

Most of the money you'll ever win at small stakes lives in this exact moment.

## The story started preflop

A continuation bet — c-bet for short — is a bet from the preflop raiser, continuing the aggression whether or not the flop helped. It works because of two things you already know.

First, range advantage: a king-high dry flop smashes the raiser's range and skips the caller's. Your specific hand missed, but your range hit — and your opponent can only respond to the range. Second, the caller misses too. Two unpaired cards miss the flop about two times in three. When they check a board that favors you, one modest bet very often ends the hand.

That's why the c-bet on the right board is the closest thing poker has to a legal cheat code: a third of the pot, offered into a range that mostly can't continue.

## The right boards, and the wrong ones

The mistake isn't c-betting too much or too little — it's c-betting every flop the same way. The board picks the plan.

C-bet relentlessly on dry, high boards: king-eight-three, ace-nine-four, queen-five-deuce rainbow. Your range owns these. Small size, near-every hand, no apologies.

Slow down on low, connected boards: seven-six-five, nine-eight-six with a flush draw. These flops crash into the caller's range — the suited connectors and small pairs they defend with. Betting your air into their best boards is donating. Check more, even with decent hands, and keep the pot small until the turn tells you more.

## How many opponents changes everything

One caller is a coin flip on whether they connected. Two callers roughly doubles the chance somebody did, and three makes it likely. The c-bet that prints against one player quietly stops working against three — every extra opponent is another range that might have hit your favourite board.

The adjustment is simple and most players never make it: against multiple callers, c-bet mostly hands with real value, and give up the pure air. Take the free card, keep the pot small, and look for a better spot. You aren't giving up an edge — you're refusing to buy one at a bad price.

## When the c-bet gets called

Half your c-bets will get called, so decide before you bet what the plan is. Calls on your good boards are usually draws and weak pairs — a second barrel on a high turn card keeps the pressure honest. But if the turn completes the obvious draw, the cheat code is over; stop firing air into a range that just improved. A c-bet is one bet with a plan attached, not a promise to bet three streets.

<KeyIdea>
  C-bet small and often on the high, dry boards your raise still owns — and check the low, connected
  ones that were never yours.
</KeyIdea>
