You c-bet the flop with ace-ten on king-nine-four. Called. The turn is a seven. You bet again. Called again. The river pairs the nine and you think: I've come this far — one more bet and they'll finally fold.

Stop. That sentence — _I've come this far_ — has cost more money than every bad beat in history combined.

## The pot doesn't remember your chips

The chips you've already put in the pot aren't yours anymore. They belong to the pot, and they'll go to whoever wins it — regardless of who contributed what. Spending more now to justify what you spent before has a name: the sunk cost fallacy, and poker punishes it every single night.

The only question that ever matters is the one in front of you: is _this_ bet, right now, a good bet? Not "have I invested too much to quit." Two lessons ago you learned the test — worse calls, or better folds. A player who called two streets on king-nine-four has a hand. Your third barrel folds out nothing that beats ace-high. The bet has no job. The answer is check, and the answer was always going to be check.

## Giving up is a profit center

Here's the reframe that makes this easier: every chip you don't spend on a hopeless bluff is identical to a chip you won. The players who lose the most at small stakes aren't the ones who bluff too little — they're the ones who can't stop firing once they've started. One saved barrel per session is thirty big blinds a month, for free.

The caller's actions are information — you learned this in the ranges lesson. Called twice means the crowd that's left is pairs and better. Believe them. The bluff that ignores information isn't aggression; it's denial with a chip count. And when a passive player suddenly raises you mid-barrel? That's the loudest information in poker. Fold and feel good about it.

## The exit plan

Build the exit before you fire the first bullet: "I'm betting this flop; I continue on high turns and give up on low ones or completed draws." Then follow it. Giving up on schedule isn't losing the hand — it's the plan working. The chips you save fold quietly into the next pot you win, and nobody at the table ever sees your best play of the night.
