The pot is ten blinds. One player bets three. Another bets eight. Most beginners see two bets. A trained eye sees two completely different sentences — because size is language, and it isn't arbitrary.

## Bets are priced in pot, not chips

First, the unit. Serious players size bets as a fraction of the pot — a third pot, half pot, three-quarters — because the pot is what sets the price of everything. A three-blind bet into ten is a nudge; the same three blinds into four is a shove-sized statement. Chips mean nothing without the pot behind them.

The fraction sets the math for the caller, too. A third-pot bet offers your opponent cheap odds — they need to win only about one time in five to call. A pot-sized bet demands they win a third of the time. Your size literally decides how correct their call is.

## Small bets: when everything is settled

Bet small — a quarter to a third of the pot — when the board is dry and the winner is unlikely to change. Remember king-seven-two rainbow? Nothing is coming. A big bet just scares off the worse hands you want to keep in. A small bet keeps every customer shopping: worse kings, sevens, curious ace-highs. Small sizes also let the board-favored range bet cheaply and often — the range-advantage play from Module 2.

## Big bets: when the meter is running

Bet big — two-thirds pot and up — when the board is wet and hands are unstable. Two jobs get done at once: you charge the flush and straight draws a losing price for their next card, and you build the pot while you're still ahead. Draws pay rent or leave — that's the whole arrangement.

## Bigger pots need bigger reasons

There's a second thing size controls that beginners rarely notice: how much money is left for later streets. Bet big on the flop and the turn bet gets big too, and the river bet after that can be your whole stack. That chain is fine when you hold a monster and want the stacks in. It's dangerous with a medium hand, because you've committed yourself to a pot your hand can't comfortably play for.

So ask one extra question before a big bet: am I happy if this becomes an all-in? If the honest answer is no, the size is too big for the hand — not because the bet is wrong, but because of where it leads.

## Size is also a tell — yours

One warning before the drills. If you bet big with strong hands and small with weak ones, observant players will read you like a menu. The fix isn't to randomize wildly — it's to size by the board, not by your hand. On this texture, this size, with everything you'd bet here. Your strong hands and your bluffs wear the same clothes, and nobody learns anything from the price tag.
